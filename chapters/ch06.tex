%========================================================= 6-1
\section*{6--1\quad INTRODUCTION}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{6--1\quad Introduction}

In the last two chapters we have been concerned with congruence of segments
only. But we also have a feeling for size of an angle---and, in fact, in
Chapter 1 we used a protractor to measure angles. If our geometry is to be
like the world of our experience, we must include in it postulates that will
enable us to compare the sizes of angles. These postulates form Postulate
Group V. There are four postulates in the group, and we take them up one at a
time. As in Chapter 4, we shall use the word ``congruent'' to give the idea of
\emph{the same size}.

%========================================================= 6-2
\section*{6--2\quad POSTULATE V--1, THE EXISTENCE POSTULATE FOR ANGLES}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{6--2\quad Postulate V--1, the existence postulate for angles}

This first postulate of Group V tells of the existence of angles congruent to
a given angle. Remember to think of the word ``congruent'' as meaning
\emph{the same size}.

\medskip
V--1 If $\ang{A}$ is not a straight angle, and if $r\VIpr$ is a ray from
$A\VIpr$ on a line $l\VIpr$, then on a given side of $l\VIpr$, there is exactly
one ray $s\VIpr$ from $A\VIpr$ such that the angle $A\VIpr$, with sides
$r\VIpr$ and $s\VIpr$, is congruent to angle $A$ (Fig.~6--1). We write this as
$\ang{A\VIpr} \cong \ang{A}$. If $\ang{A}$ is a straight angle, then the
straight angle at $A\VIpr$, with one side $r\VIpr$, is the only angle with one
side $r\VIpr$ congruent to angle $A$.
\medskip

\section*{6--3\quad POSTULATE V--2, THE THREE-PART ANGLE POSTULATE}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{6--3\quad Postulate V--2, the three-part angle postulate}

The next postulate is for angles what Postulate III--2 is for segments. It may
seem odd to have to state such seemingly obvious things, but remember we are
dealing with those imaginary things called points and lines, and so we must
state \emph{all} the properties we need.

\medskip
\noindent
\begin{tabular}{@{}p{2.2em}@{}p{0.86\linewidth}@{}}
V--2 & (a)\ \ $\ang{A} \cong \ang{A}$.\ \ (Congruence of angles is reflexive.)\\[2pt]
 & (b)\ \ If $\ang{A} \cong \ang{B}$, then $\ang{B} \cong \ang{A}$.
   (Congruence of angles is symmetric.)\\[2pt]
 & (c)\ \ If $\ang{A} \cong \ang{B}$, and $\ang{B} \cong \ang{C}$, then
   $\ang{A} \cong \ang{C}$. (Congruence of angles is transitive.)
\end{tabular}
\medskip

%========================================================= 6-4
\section*{6--4\quad POSTULATE V--3, THE ADDITION AND SUBTRACTION OF ANGLES POSTULATE}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{6--4\quad Postulate V--3, the addition and subtraction of angles postulate}

The next postulate does for angles what Postulate III--3 does for segments. It
enables us to ``add'' and ``subtract'' angles. To avoid a complicated statement
in words, we explain the postulate by using Fig.~6--5, where the side common to
$\ang{B}$ and $\ang{C}$, except the vertex, is in the interior of $\ang{A}$,
and the same for $\ang{A\VIpr}$, $\ang{B\VIpr}$, and $\ang{C\VIpr}$.

\FIGVIFIVE

\medskip
\noindent
V--3 If the angles in any two of the pairs $\ang{A}$ and $\ang{A\VIpr}$,
$\ang{B}$ and $\ang{B\VIpr}$, $\ang{C}$ and $\ang{C\VIpr}$ are congruent to
each other, then the angles of the third pair are also congruent to each other.
In other words:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*), leftmargin=2.6em, itemsep=1pt, topsep=3pt]
\item If $\ang{A} \cong \ang{A\VIpr}$ and $\ang{B} \cong \ang{B\VIpr}$, then
  $\ang{C} \cong \ang{C\VIpr}$.
\item If $\ang{B} \cong \ang{B\VIpr}$ and $\ang{C} \cong \ang{C\VIpr}$, then
  $\ang{A} \cong \ang{A\VIpr}$.
\item If $\ang{C} \cong \ang{C\VIpr}$ and $\ang{A} \cong \ang{A\VIpr}$, then
  $\ang{B} \cong \ang{B\VIpr}$.
\end{enumerate}
\noindent The angle $A$ may be a straight angle.
\medskip

Supplementary and adjacent angles were defined in Definition 3--9. The
statement that $\ang{A}$ is \emph{supplementary} to $\ang{B}$ shall mean that
$\ang{A}$ is congruent to an angle that is \emph{supplementary and adjacent} to
$\ang{B}$.

\begin{theorem}
If two angles are congruent to each other, then their supplements are also
congruent to each other.
\end{theorem}

\emph{Proof.} Let the given angles be $A$ and $A\VIpr$, and let their
supplements be $B$ and $B\VIpr$, respectively, as in Fig.~6--10. We are given
that $\ang{A} \cong \ang{A\VIpr}$. Since any two straight angles are congruent
to each other, it follows at once from Postulate V--3 that $\ang{B} \cong
\ang{B\VIpr}$. (Why?)

\FIGVITENELEVENTWELVE

\begin{theorem}
Angles that are vertical to each other are also congruent to each other.
\end{theorem}

\emph{Proof.} Let the angles $A$ and $A\VIpr$ be vertical to each other, as in
Fig.~6--11, and consider $\ang{B}$, one of the other angles formed by the
lines. Then $\ang{B}$ is the supplement of $\ang{A}$ and also the supplement of
$\ang{A\VIpr}$. Since $\ang{B} \cong \ang{B}$, it follows from Theorem 6--1
that $\ang{A} \cong \ang{A\VIpr}$.

\begin{definition}
An angle (Fig.~6--12) that is congruent to its supplement is called a
\emph{right angle}.
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}
Note that the words \emph{congruent}, \emph{vertical}, \emph{equal}, and
\emph{supplementary} refer to relations between two objects, not to their
properties. That is, it makes no sense to say that $\ang{A}$ is a vertical
angle, or a congruent angle, or a supplementary angle. At times, we refer to
two angles $A$ and $B$ as equal angles, or vertical angles, or congruent
angles, etc. What we really mean is that $\ang{A}$ is equal to $\ang{B}$,
$\ang{A}$ is vertical to $\ang{B}$, $\ang{A}$ is congruent to $\ang{B}$, etc.
It is important to distinguish between properties and relations.
\end{remark}

\section*{6--5\quad POSTULATE V--4, THE SIDE-ANGLE-SIDE POSTULATE}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{6--5\quad Postulate V--4, the side-angle-side postulate}

This postulate (abbreviated S.A.S.) is one that combines congruence of segments
with congruence of angles. Up to this point, we have the two kinds of
congruence but no relationship between them. This is the connecting link that
will enable us to prove many theorems.

\medskip
\noindent
V--4 If two triangles, $\tri{ABC}$ and $\tri{A\VIpr B\VIpr C\VIpr}$, are such
that $AB \cong A\VIpr B\VIpr$, $AC \cong A\VIpr C\VIpr$, and $\ang{A} \cong
\ang{A\VIpr}$, then $\ang{B} \cong \ang{B\VIpr}$ and $\ang{C} \cong
\ang{C\VIpr}$ (Fig.~6--16).
\medskip

\FIGVISIXTEEN

This postulate tells us that if two triangles have two sides of one congruent
to two sides of the other, and if the angles formed by these two sides (called
the \emph{included} angles) are congruent to each other, then the other pairs
of corresponding angles are congruent.

\section*{6--6\quad ISOSCELES TRIANGLES}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{6--6\quad Isosceles triangles}

We can now prove a theorem about isosceles triangles, for which we need a
definition. The definition of isosceles triangle is the same as that given in
Chapter 1, Exercise Group 1--4:

\begin{definition}
A triangle is called \emph{isosceles} if two of its sides are congruent (of
equal length). The \emph{base angles} of the triangle are those opposite the
congruent sides.
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}
In every isosceles triangle, the base angles are congruent to each other.
\end{theorem}

\emph{Proof.} Consider any isosceles triangle, and label the vertices in two
different ways, as shown in Fig.~6--32. Let $AB \cong AC$. Let us look at
the triangle both as $\tri{ABC}$ and as $\tri{A\VIpr B\VIpr C\VIpr}$. We have
$AB \cong AC \cong A\VIpr B\VIpr$, and $AC \cong AB = A\VIpr C\VIpr$, and
$\ang{A} \cong \ang{A\VIpr}$. Therefore, by the S.A.S. postulate, $\ang{B}
\cong \ang{B\VIpr}$, that is, $\ang{B} \cong \ang{C}$.

This proof is quite interesting because it illustrates very well how good
notation can help. In the proof we look at the same triangle in two different
ways, and once we have done this, the proof is very short.

\section*{6--7\quad CONGRUENCE OF TRIANGLES}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{6--7\quad Congruence of triangles}

We now define congruence for triangles and prove some theorems.

\begin{definition}
Two triangles are said to be \emph{congruent} to each other if they can be
labeled $\tri{ABC}$ and $\tri{A\VIpr B\VIpr C\VIpr}$, such that
$AB \cong A\VIpr B\VIpr$, $AC \cong A\VIpr C\VIpr$, $BC \cong B\VIpr C\VIpr$,
$\ang{A} \cong \ang{A\VIpr}$, $\ang{B} \cong \ang{B\VIpr}$, and $\ang{C} \cong
\ang{C\VIpr}$. We then write $\tri{ABC} \cong \tri{A\VIpr B\VIpr C\VIpr}$ and
read this as ``triangle $ABC$ is congruent to triangle $A\VIpr B\VIpr
C\VIpr$.'' $\ang{A}$ corresponds to $\ang{A\VIpr}$, $\ang{B}$ to
$\ang{B\VIpr}$, side $AC$ to side $A\VIpr C\VIpr$, etc.
\end{definition}

\exercise{6--7}
Prove that congruence of triangles is transitive.

\begin{theorem}
If the vertices of two triangles may be labeled $A$, $B$, $C$ and $A\VIpr$,
$B\VIpr$, $C\VIpr$, such that $AB \cong A\VIpr B\VIpr$, $AC \cong A\VIpr
C\VIpr$, $\ang{A} \cong \ang{A\VIpr}$, then $\tri{ABC} \cong \tri{A\VIpr
B\VIpr C\VIpr}$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{remark}
This is the first of the three congruence theorems for triangles. It may also
be stated as follows: \emph{if two triangles are such that two sides and the
included angle of one are congruent, respectively, to two sides and the
included angle of the other, then the triangles are congruent.} This theorem
is often called the ``side-angle-side theorem'' and is abbreviated S.A.S.
\end{remark}

\emph{Proof.} From the S.A.S. postulate, we know that $\ang{B} \cong
\ang{B\VIpr}$ and $\ang{C} \cong \ang{C\VIpr}$. It only remains to be shown
that the third sides are congruent, that is, that $BC \cong B\VIpr C\VIpr$. We
prove this by considering the point $D\VIpr$ on ray $C\VIpr B\VIpr$, such that
$CB \cong C\VIpr D\VIpr$, as in Fig.~6--43, and then showing that $D\VIpr =
B\VIpr$.

\begin{steps}
\step{There is a point $D\VIpr$ on the ray $C\VIpr B\VIpr$ such that $CB \cong
      C\VIpr D\VIpr$.}{Existence postulate for segments.}
\step{$AC \cong A\VIpr C\VIpr$, and $AB \cong A\VIpr B\VIpr$.}{Given.}
\step{$\ang{B\VIpr A\VIpr C\VIpr} \cong \ang{BAC}$.}{Given.}
\step{$\ang{C} \cong \ang{C\VIpr}$, and $\ang{B} \cong \ang{B\VIpr}$.}{S.A.S. postulate.}
\step{$\ang{BAC} \cong \ang{D\VIpr A\VIpr C\VIpr}$.}{By S.A.S. postulate, considering
      $\tri{ACB}$ and $\tri{A\VIpr C\VIpr D\VIpr}$.}
\step{Ray $A\VIpr B\VIpr$ is the same as the ray $A\VIpr D\VIpr$.}{Existence postulate for angles.}
\step{$B\VIpr = D\VIpr$.}{The lines $A\VIpr B\VIpr$ and
      $B\VIpr C\VIpr$ intersect in exactly one point by Theorem 3--1. This
      point is $B\VIpr$ and is also $D\VIpr$.}
\step{$BC \cong B\VIpr C\VIpr$.}{Statements 1 and 7.}
\step{$\tri{ABC} \cong \tri{A\VIpr B\VIpr C\VIpr}$.}{Definition 6--4.}
\end{steps}

In the exercises that follow we will make use of the congruence theorems. In
some exercises it will be convenient to know that \emph{``halves'' of congruent
segments are congruent to each other}. That is, if $AB \cong CD$ and $M$ and $N$
are midpoints of $AB$ and $CD$ respectively, then $AM \cong CN$. To see this we
note that $\sg{AB} = \sg{CD}$ and by the properties of numbers $\sg{AM} =

\section*{6--8\quad THE SECOND CONGRUENCE THEOREM}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{6--8\quad The second congruence theorem}

The first congruence theorem was the side-angle-side theorem (S.A.S.). The
second one says that two triangles are congruent if two angles and the included
side of one are, respectively, congruent to two angles and the included side of
the other. This theorem is called the ``angle-side-angle theorem'' and is
abbreviated A.S.A.

\begin{theorem}
If two triangles $\tri{ABC}$ and $\tri{A\VIpr B\VIpr C\VIpr}$ are such that
$\ang{A} \cong \ang{A\VIpr}$, $\ang{C} \cong \ang{C\VIpr}$, and $AC \cong
A\VIpr C\VIpr$, then $\tri{ABC} \cong \tri{A\VIpr B\VIpr C\VIpr}$.
\end{theorem}

\FIGVIFIFTYFIVE

\emph{Proof.} We consider the point $D\VIpr$ on the ray $A\VIpr B\VIpr$
(Fig.~6--55), such that $AB \cong A\VIpr D\VIpr$. Then it is true that
$\tri{ABC} \cong \tri{A\VIpr D\VIpr C\VIpr}$. (Why?) Then, if $D\VIpr \neq
B\VIpr$, we have both $\ang{D\VIpr C\VIpr A\VIpr}$ and $\ang{B\VIpr C\VIpr
A\VIpr}$ congruent to $\ang{C}$. This is impossible, so $B\VIpr = D\VIpr$.

\begin{steps}
\step{There is a point $D\VIpr$ on the ray $A\VIpr B\VIpr$, such that $AB \cong
      A\VIpr D\VIpr$.}{Existence postulate for segments.}
\step{$\ang{A} \cong \ang{A\VIpr}$, $AC \cong A\VIpr C\VIpr$.}{Given.}
\step{$\ang{C} \cong \ang{D\VIpr C\VIpr A\VIpr}$.}{S.A.S. postulate, applied to
      $\tri{ABC}$ and $\tri{A\VIpr D\VIpr C\VIpr}$.}
\step{$\ang{C} \cong \ang{B\VIpr C\VIpr A\VIpr}$.}{Given.}
\step{Ray $C\VIpr D\VIpr$ $=$ ray $C\VIpr B\VIpr$.}{Existence postulate for angles.}
\step{$D\VIpr = B\VIpr$.}{Two lines intersect in at most
      one point.}
\step{$\tri{ABC} \cong \tri{A\VIpr B\VIpr C\VIpr}$.}{S.A.S. theorem.}
\end{steps}

\section*{6--9\quad THE THIRD CONGRUENCE THEOREM}

The two previous congruence theorems were the S.A.S. and A.S.A. theorems. The
third of the three fundamental congruence theorems states that two triangles
are congruent if the sides of one are congruent to the sides of the other. It
is called the ``side-side-side theorem'' and is abbreviated S.S.S.

\section*{6--10\quad RIGHT ANGLES}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{6--10\quad Right angles}

In Definition 6--1 we defined right angles, but so far \emph{we do not know
that there are such angles}. We now show how to use our congruence theorem
(S.A.S.) to show that there is a right angle in our geometry.

\FIGVININETYTHREE

\emph{Proof.} On a line $l$, choose a point $O$ and let $\ang{AOB}$ be an angle
(not straight) with one side on $l$ (Fig.~6--93). Now consider the angle
congruent to $\ang{AOB}$, with vertex $O$, one side $OB$ on the line $l$ and
the other on the opposite side of $l$ from $A$. Choose the point $A\VIpr$ on
this last side, such that $OA \cong OA\VIpr$. Because $A$ and $A\VIpr$ are on
opposite sides of $l$, the segment $AA\VIpr$ intersects the line in some point
$C$. There are three cases to consider, as shown in Fig.~6--94.

\FIGVININETYFOUR

If $C$ is on the same side of $O$ as $B$, then by the S.A.S. theorem,
$\tri{AOC} \cong \tri{A\VIpr OC}$. Hence $\ang{OCA} \cong \ang{OCA\VIpr}$.
Since these angles are congruent and supplementary to each other, they are
right angles. In case $C = O$, we have $\ang{AOB} \cong \ang{A\VIpr OB}$, and
hence they are right angles. If $C$ is on the opposite side of $O$ from $B$,
then the angles $AOC$ and $A\VIpr OC$ are congruent. (Why?) We may apply the
S.A.S. congruence theorem. $\ang{ACO} \cong \ang{A\VIpr CO}$, and these are
right angles.

\section*{6--11\quad INEQUALITIES FOR ANGLES}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{6--11\quad Inequalities for angles}

Even before studying geometry you used angles of the same size and compared
angles of different sizes (by saying, for example, that one angle is larger
than another). In this section, we see how to compare the sizes of angles. To
do this, we have to define $>$ (is greater than) and $<$ (is less than) for
angles. The idea we use in forming this definition is illustrated in
Fig.~6--97. If $\ang{B\VIpr}$ is congruent to $\ang{B}$, and $\ang{B\VIpr}$ and
$\ang{A}$ are as drawn (that is, the other side of $\ang{B\VIpr}$ is \emph{in
the interior} of $\ang{A}$), then we would say that $\ang{B} < \ang{A}$. In
Fig.~6--97, we have, so to speak, ``moved'' $\ang{B}$ to the position of
$\ang{B\VIpr}$ for comparison. Notice how the definition below parallels the
definition of ``greater than'' and ``less than'' for segments.

\FIGVININETYSEVEN

\begin{definition}
Let $A$ and $B$ be any angles not straight angles. Let $\ang{B\VIpr}$ be an
angle congruent to $\ang{B}$, such that one side of $\ang{B\VIpr}$ is the same
as the first side of $\ang{A}$, and the other side of $\ang{B\VIpr}$ and the
second side of $\ang{A}$ both lie on the same side of the first side of
$\ang{A}$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*), leftmargin=2.2em, itemsep=2pt, topsep=3pt]
\item If the second side of $\ang{B\VIpr}$ is interior to $\ang{A}$, we say
  that $\ang{B} < \ang{A}$. (See Fig.~6--98.)
\item If the second side of $\ang{B\VIpr}$ is exterior to $\ang{A}$, we say
  that $\ang{B} > \ang{A}$. (See Fig.~6--99.)
\item If $\ang{A}$ is a straight angle, and $\ang{B}$ is not, we say that
  $\ang{A} > \ang{B}$ and that $\ang{B} < \ang{A}$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{definition}

\FIGVININETYEIGHTNINETYNINE

Our intuition tells us that if $\ang{B} < \ang{A}$, then $\ang{A} > \ang{B}$,
and if $\ang{B} > \ang{A}$, then $\ang{A} < \ang{B}$. These statements require
proof. Since the proofs of these statements are essentially the same, we prove
only the first.

\begin{theorem}
If $\ang{B}$ and $\ang{A}$ are two angles, such that $\ang{B} < \ang{A}$, then
$\ang{A} > \ang{B}$.
\end{theorem}

\VIstarnote{*This proof is difficult. If the teacher wishes, Theorem 6--8 may
be treated as a postulate.}

\stex\emph{Proof.} Figure 6--100 shows that $\ang{B} < \ang{A}$. To show that
$\ang{A} > \ang{B}$, we must show that the second side of $\ang{A\VIpr}$ is in
the exterior of $\ang{B}$, as in Fig.~6--101.

\FIGVIONEHUNDREDONEHUNDREDONE

\FIGVIONEHUNDREDTWO

Choose points $D$ and $E$ on the sides of $\ang{A}$ and points $D\VIpr$ and
$E\VIpr$ on the sides of $\ang{A\VIpr}$ such that $AD \cong BD\VIpr$ and $AE
\cong BE\VIpr$. (See Fig.~6--102.) $DE$ intersects the second side of
$\ang{B\VIpr}$ in some point $F$ between $D$ and $E$ because the second side of
$\ang{B\VIpr}$ is in the interior of $\ang{A}$. $\tri{ADE} \cong \tri{BD\VIpr
E\VIpr}$ by the S.A.S. theorem, and hence $DE \cong D\VIpr E\VIpr$. Choose
$F\VIpr$ between $D\VIpr$ and $E\VIpr$, such that $DF \cong D\VIpr F\VIpr$.
This is possible because $D\VIpr E\VIpr \cong DE > DF$. The ray $BF\VIpr$ is in
the interior of $\ang{A\VIpr}$. (Why?) $\ang{D} \cong \ang{D\VIpr}$, and hence
$\tri{FDA} \cong \tri{F\VIpr D\VIpr B}$. $\ang{DAF} \cong \ang{D\VIpr
BF\VIpr}$, and $\ang{DAF}$ was given congruent to $\ang{B}$. By the three-part
angle postulate, $\ang{B} \cong \ang{D\VIpr BF\VIpr}$; it follows that the ray
$BF\VIpr$ is the same as the second side of $\ang{B}$. Therefore, the second
side of $\ang{B}$ is interior to $\ang{A\VIpr}$ or, what is the same, the
second side of $\ang{A\VIpr}$ is in the exterior of $\ang{B}$. This completes
the proof that $\ang{A} > \ang{B}$.

\begin{remark}
Theorem 6--8 tells us that we get the same result regardless of whether we
``move'' $B$ to compare with $A$, or ``move'' $A$ to compare with $B$.
\end{remark}

\begin{theorem}
For any two angles $\ang{A}$ and $\ang{B}$, either $\ang{A} < \ang{B}$, or
$\ang{A} \cong \ang{B}$, or $\ang{A} > \ang{B}$; and exactly one of these is
true.
\end{theorem}

\emph{Proof.} If $\ang{A}$ is a straight angle, either $\ang{A} \cong \ang{B}$
and $\ang{B}$ is also a straight angle or, by Definition 6--5, $\ang{A} >
\ang{B}$. Similarly, if $\ang{B}$ is a straight angle, either $\ang{B} \cong
\ang{A}$ or $\ang{B} > \ang{A}$. If neither $\ang{A}$ nor $\ang{B}$ is
straight, then the second side of $\ang{B\VIpr}$ coincides with the second side
of $\ang{A}$, and $\ang{B} \cong \ang{A}$; or the second side of $\ang{B\VIpr}$
is interior to $\ang{A}$, and $\ang{A} > \ang{B}$; or the second side of
$\ang{B\VIpr}$ is exterior to $\ang{A}$, and $\ang{A} < \ang{B}$. Since only
one of these possibilities can hold, the proof is complete.

Some simple properties of inequalities are collected in the following theorem.

\begin{theorem}
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*), leftmargin=2.2em, itemsep=1pt, topsep=3pt]
\item If $\ang{A} \cong \ang{B}$, and $\ang{B} < \ang{C}$, then $\ang{A} < \ang{C}$.
\item If $\ang{A} < \ang{B}$, and $\ang{B} \cong \ang{C}$, then $\ang{A} < \ang{C}$.
\item If $\ang{A} \cong \ang{B}$, and $\ang{B} > \ang{C}$, then $\ang{A} > \ang{C}$.
\item If $\ang{A} < \ang{B}$, and $\ang{B} < \ang{C}$, then $\ang{A} < \ang{C}$.
\item If $\ang{A} \geqq \ang{B}$, and $\ang{B} \geqq \ang{C}$, then
  $\ang{A} \geqq \ang{C}$.*
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

\VIstarnote{*The symbol $\geqq$ means ``greater than or congruent to,'' as in
Chapter 4.}

\emph{Proof of} (a). Since $\ang{B} < \ang{C}$, on ``moving'' $\ang{B}$ to
compare with $\ang{C}$, the second side of $\ang{B\VIpr}$ is interior to
$\ang{C}$. Since $\ang{A} \cong \ang{B}$, the second sides of $\ang{A\VIpr}$
and $\ang{B\VIpr}$ would be the same. Hence $\ang{A} < \ang{C}$. The proofs in
all the other cases are essentially the same.

\section*{6--12\quad RIGHT ANGLES AGAIN}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{6--12\quad Right angles again}

We can now use Theorem 6--9 to prove that all right angles are congruent to
each other. It is interesting, from a historical point of view, to know that
Euclid \emph{postulated} this fact. The proof we give below could have been
formulated by Euclid just as well as he formulated other proofs. Not only did
Euclid ignore postulates that were needed (like the betweenness postulates),
but he also introduced postulates that were not needed.

\begin{theorem}
All right angles are congruent to each other.
\end{theorem}

\emph{Proof.} Suppose that two right angles are $\ang{A}$ and $\ang{B}$, and
that their supplements are $\ang{A\VIpr}$ and $\ang{B\VIpr}$, as in
Fig.~6--103(a) and 6--103(b). There are only three possibilities, according to
Theorem 6--9. Either $\ang{A} < \ang{B}$, or $\ang{A} \cong \ang{B}$, or
$\ang{A} > \ang{B}$, and one of these must be true. We will show that $\ang{A}
< \ang{B}$ and $\ang{A} > \ang{B}$ are false. Suppose that $\ang{A} < \ang{B}$,
and that $\ang{B\VIppr}$ is congruent to $\ang{B}$ and located as in
Fig.~6--103(c). Let $B\VIpppr$ be the supplement of $\ang{B\VIppr}$.

\FIGVIONEHUNDREDTHREE

\begin{steps}
\step{$\ang{B\VIppr} \cong \ang{B}$.}{By definition of $\ang{B\VIppr}$.}
\step{$\ang{B\VIpppr}$ is the supplement of $\ang{B\VIppr}$, and
      $\ang{B\VIpr}$ is the supplement of $\ang{B}$.}{By definition of supplement.}
\step{$\ang{B\VIpr} \cong \ang{B\VIpppr}$.}{If angles are congruent, their
      supplements are congruent.}
\step{$\ang{B} \cong \ang{B\VIpr}$.}{$\ang{B}$ is a right angle.}
\step{$\ang{B} \cong \ang{B\VIpppr}$.}{Angle congruence is transitive.}
\step{$\ang{A} < \ang{B}$.}{Hypothesis.}
\step{$\ang{A} < \ang{B\VIpppr}$.}{$\ang{A} < \ang{B} \cong
      \ang{B\VIpppr}$.}
\step{$\ang{B\VIpppr} < \ang{A\VIpr}$.}{The ``second'' side of
      $\ang{B\VIpppr}$ is in the interior of $\ang{A\VIpr}$.}
\step{$\ang{B\VIpppr} < \ang{A}$.}{$\ang{B\VIpppr} < \ang{A\VIpr}
      \cong \ang{A}$.}
\step{$\ang{A} < \ang{A}$.}{Statements 7 and 9.}
\end{steps}

Since $\ang{A} \cong \ang{A}$, we know by Theorem 6--9 that it is impossible
for $\ang{A}$ to be less than itself. Therefore, it is impossible that $\ang{A}
< \ang{B}$. In the same way, it can be shown that it is impossible that
$\ang{B} < \ang{A}$. Therefore, we must have $\ang{A} \cong \ang{B}$. This
completes the proof.

\begin{definition}
Two lines, $l$ and $m$, are said to be \emph{perpendicular} to each other if
they intersect and the angles formed are right angles, as in Fig.~6--104(a).
To indicate that lines are perpendicular we write ``$l \perp m$.''
\end{definition}

\FIGVIONEHUNDREDFOUR

The line $l$ is called the \emph{perpendicular bisector} of the segment $AB$ if
$l \perp AB$ and $l$ passes through the midpoint of $AB$, as in
Fig.~6--104(b). This is written ``$l \perp$ bis $AB$,'' and read ``$l$ is the
perpendicular bisector of $AB$.''
