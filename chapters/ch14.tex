\section*{14--1\quad INTRODUCTION}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{14--1\quad Introduction}

In this chapter we outline the geometry of space, that is, three-dimensional
Euclidean space. Sometimes this study is called \emph{solid} geometry. Just as
plane geometry is an abstraction derived from our experience with flat surfaces,
so solid geometry is an abstraction derived from our ordinary perception of
objects in space.

In this chapter you will find many figures and many theorems and definitions,
but very few proofs. Proofs are omitted because this chapter is intended to be
only an introduction to the subject. Nevertheless, if you study the theorems in
the order they occur, you will find that their arrangement is logical because
they can be proved in that order. Furthermore, most of them have fairly simple
proofs. To get an understanding of the \emph{facts} of solid geometry, it will
be sufficient to read over the theorems and be sure that you know what they say.

\section*{14--3\quad THE POSTULATES}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{14--3\quad The Postulates}

It is perhaps somewhat surprising that most of the essential difficulties in
geometry already occur in the plane, so that extension to space geometry
requires no fundamentally new concepts. As a matter of fact, very few more
postulates are needed than for the plane, indeed only four more.

\begin{postulate}{VII--1}
There is a set of points called \emph{space}. Certain subsets of these points
are called planes. There are at least four points that are not in any one plane.
\end{postulate}

\begin{postulate}{VII--2}
There is exactly one plane containing any three noncollinear points
(Fig.~14--3).
\end{postulate}

\FIGXIVTHREE

\begin{postulate}{VII--3}
Each plane satisfies all the postulates for plane geometry. In particular, the
line joining two points of a plane lies in that plane. Furthermore, the
postulates for congruence of segments and angles apply whether or not the
segments and angles are in the same plane.
\end{postulate}

\begin{postulate}{VII--4}
Each plane separates space. By this we mean that the set of points of space not
on the plane consists of two subsets, called the sides of the plane, with the
following properties. If two points are in the same subset, then the segment
joining them does not meet the plane, but if the two points are in different
subsets, the segment joining them does intersect the plane.
\end{postulate}

Postulate VII--1 asserts that no plane fills up all of space, so that we have a
geometry of more than two dimensions (Fig.~14--4).

Postulate VII--2 gives us a simple requirement for fixing a definite plane.

Postulate VII--3 states that our planes are as we want them to be from our study
of plane geometry. In addition, this postulate relates congruent lines and
angles in different planes.

Postulate VII--4 expresses the familiar fact that a wall between neighbors
separates them.

\FIGXIVFOURFIVE

\exercise{14--2}
Restate Postulate Group VII in your own words.

From these postulates and the first two postulate groups for plane geometry, we
can prove a number of theorems concerning intersections of lines and planes.

\begin{theorem}
If a line $l$ does not lie in a plane $\pi$, and has at least one point in
$\pi$, then $l$ and $\pi$ have exactly one point in common (Fig.~14--5).
\end{theorem}

For the rest of the chapter, proofs will be left out, but we include a proof of
Theorem 14--1 as a guide to start you out correctly.

\noindent\emph{Proof.} There is, by assumption, at least one point $P$ in both
$l$ and $\pi$. Suppose that there were a second point $Q$. Then, by Postulate
VII--3, the line $l = PQ$ would lie in the plane $\pi$. But we have assumed that
$l$ is not in $\pi$. Hence $P$ is the only point in both $l$ and $\pi$.

\begin{theorem}
There is a unique plane containing a given line and a point not on the line
(Fig.~14--6).
\end{theorem}

\begin{theorem}

\end{theorem}

\noindent Figure 14--7 may suggest a proof.

\begin{theorem}
There is a unique plane containing two intersecting lines (Fig.~14--8).
\end{theorem}

\begin{theorem}
If two distinct planes have at least one point in common, they have precisely a
line in common (Fig.~14--9).
\end{theorem}

\FIGXIVEIGHTNINE

\begin{definition}
If two planes $\pi_1$ and $\pi_2$ intersect in a line $l$ (Fig.~14--10), let
$\pi_1^+$ and $\pi_2^+$ be half planes of $\pi_1$ and $\pi_2$, determined by
$l$. The set of all points in $\pi_1^+$ and $\pi_2^+$ is called a
\emph{dihedral angle}. The line $l$ is called the \emph{edge}, and $\pi_1^+$ and
$\pi_2^+$ are called the \emph{faces}, of the dihedral angle.
\end{definition}

\FIGXIVTEN

\begin{definition}
The interior of the dihedral angle with faces $\pi_1^+$ and $\pi_2^+$ is the set
of all points of space on the same side of $\pi_2$ as $\pi_1^+$ and on the same
side of $\pi_1$ as $\pi_2^+$.
\end{definition}

\section*{14--4\quad PERPENDICULAR LINES\\AND PLANES}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{14--4\quad Perpendicular Lines and Planes}

Before giving some theorems, we observe that all right angles are congruent
regardless of whether they are in the same plane. We know this because our
postulates for angle congruence state that, given an $\ang{AOB}$ in a plane
$\pi$ and a ray $r'$ from $O'$ in a plane $\pi'$, there is exactly one ray $r''$
from $O'$ (on a given side of the line of $r'$ in the plane $\pi'$) that forms
with $r'$ an angle congruent to $\ang{AOB}$. See Fig.~14--13.

\FIGXIVTHIRTEENFOURTEEN

\begin{definition}
A line $l$ is perpendicular to a plane $\pi$ if $l$ intersects $\pi$ at a point
$P$ and $l$ is perpendicular to every line in $\pi$ that passes through $P$
(Fig.~14--14).
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}
If a line $l$ is perpendicular to two intersecting lines, then it is
perpendicular to the plane containing the lines (Fig.~14--15).
\end{theorem}

\noindent[\emph{Hint:} Choose $A$ and $A'$ such that $AP \cong A'P$. Choose $B$
and $C$. Then prove that $\tri{ABC} \cong \tri{A'BC}$. Then prove that
$\tri{ABQ} \cong \tri{A'BQ}$, so that $AQ \cong A'Q$. This will imply that line
$m \perp l$.]

\FIGXIVFIFTEENSIXTEEN

\begin{theorem}
There is exactly one plane perpendicular to a line at a point of the line.
\end{theorem}

\noindent See Fig.~14--16(a).

\noindent[\emph{Hint:} Use the previous theorem to show that there is at least
one plane. To prove there is only one, it is sufficient to show that if $l_1$,
$l_2$, and $l_3$ in Fig.~14--16(b) are distinct lines perpendicular to $l$ at
$P$, then they are in a single plane. Thus, the plane of $l_2$ and $l_3$ meets
the plane of $l$ and $l_1$ in a line $l' \perp l$. Since there is only one line
in the plane of $l$ and $l_1$ perpendicular to $l$ at $P$, we have $l_1 = l'$.
Hence $l_1$, $l_2$, and $l_3$ are coplanar.]

\begin{theorem}
There is exactly one plane perpendicular to a line and containing a point not on
the line (Fig.~14--17).
\end{theorem}

\section*{14--5\quad POLYHEDRA AND\\POLYHEDRAL ANGLES}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{14--5\quad Polyhedra and Polyhedral Angles}

\begin{definition}
Let $P_1P_2\ldots P_n$ be a simple closed polygon in a plane $\pi$, and let $V$
be a point not in $\pi$, as in Fig.~14--24. The set of points $Q$ on rays from
$V$ through all the points $P$ of the polygon is called a polyhedral angle. $V$
is the \emph{vertex}, and $VP_1$, $VP_2, \ldots VP_n$ are called \emph{edges}.
The planes $VP_1P_2$, $VP_2P_3, \ldots$ contain the \emph{faces} of the angle.
The angles $\ang{P_1VP_2}$, $\ang{P_2VP_3}, \ldots$ are called \emph{face
angles}. The polyhedral angle is called convex if the polygon $P_1 \ldots P_n$
is convex.
\end{definition}

\exercisegroup{14--6}
\begin{exercisecols}
\begin{exlist}
\item Draw a trihedral angle and a tetrahedral angle. These have three and four
      faces, respectively.
\item Can a trihedral angle have three right-angled face angles?
\end{exlist}
\end{exercisecols}

\begin{theorem}
The sum\textsuperscript{*} of two face angles of a trihedral angle is greater
than the third face angle (Fig.~14--25).
\end{theorem}

\XIVnote{*\,The word \emph{sum} is not defined, but the idea is clear.}

\noindent[\emph{Hint:} It suffices to prove the theorem in the case in which the
third angle is the largest. Suppose that that angle is $\ang{AVB}$, as in
Fig.~14--25. Choose $C$ such that $\ang{CAV} \cong \ang{BAV}$. Choose $D$ on
$AB$ such that $AC \cong AD$. Now show that $VC \cong VD$ and $CB > DB$. Hence
conclude that $\ang{CVB} > \ang{DVB}$.]

\FIGXIVTWENTYFOURTWENTYFIVE

\begin{theorem}
The sum of the face angles of a convex polyhedral angle is less than four right
angles.
\end{theorem}

\FIGXIVTWENTYSIXTWENTYSEVEN

\begin{definition}
A \emph{polygonal cell} (Fig.~14--27) is the sets of points on, or interior to,
a simple plane polygon $P_1P_2\ldots P_{n-1}$. The edges of the cell are the
sides $P_1P_2$, $P_2P_3, \ldots$ of the polygon.
\end{definition}

A \emph{polyhedron} is the set of points on a finite number of polygonal cells
joined together in the following way:

\begin{enumerate}[label=(\arabic*), leftmargin=2.1em, itemsep=2pt, topsep=3pt]
\item Any two cells have either no points in common, exactly one vertex in
      common, or exactly one edge in common.
\item Every edge is on precisely two cells.
\end{enumerate}

\exercise{14--8}
With Scotch tape and cardboard, construct a number of polyhedra.

\begin{theorem}
A polyhedron separates space in the following sense. The points not on the
polyhedron consist of two sets (called the inside and the outside) such that a
polygonal path joining an inside point and an outside point must intersect the
polyhedron, and two inside (or two outside) points can be joined by a polygonal
path not intersecting the polyhedron.
\end{theorem}

\exercise{14--9}
Describe the interior of a tetrahedron.

\begin{definition}
A polyhedron is called \emph{convex} if the segment joining any two points of
the polyhedron lies either in the polyhedron or its interior. A polyhedron is
\emph{regular} if it is convex and all faces are congruent regular polygons.
\end{definition}

triangles; squares; pentagons.

\begin{theorem}
There are but five (except for size) regular polyhedra (Fig.~14--28).
\end{theorem}

\section*{14--8\quad VOLUME}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{14--8\quad Volume}

Just as area in the plane offers special difficulties, so does volume in space.
We content ourselves with listing the theorems and sketching a few ``proofs,''
obtained by drawing figures. Proofs that involve the notion of limit are not
presented.

\begin{theorem}
The volume of a rectangular parallelepiped (box) is equal to the product of the
lengths of three concurrent edges. Volume $=lwh$.
\end{theorem}

\noindent See Fig.~14--34.

\FIGXIVTHIRTYFOURTHIRTYFIVE

\begin{theorem}
If a polyhedron is the union of two other polyhedra, with only a face and no
interior points in common, then its volume is the sum of the volumes of the two
polyhedra forming it (Fig.~14--35).
\end{theorem}

The volume of a parallelepiped is equal to the area of a base multiplied by the
corresponding altitude (Fig.~14--37).
\end{theorem}

the theorem for prisms with triangular bases.
