\section*{9--1\quad INTRODUCTION}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{9--1\quad Introduction}

In this chapter we study relations between two polygons that have ``the same
shape.'' (Later on we explain precisely what we mean by ``the same shape,'' and
say that such figures are \emph{similar to each other}.) Then we prove some
theorems about similar triangles, and using these theorems we prove the
Pythagorean theorem, which states that if $a$ and $b$ are lengths of the sides of
a right triangle, and $c$ is the length of the hypotenuse, then $c^2 = a^2 + b^2$.
We see as we study this chapter, that the theorems on similar triangles depend
very much on the properties of parallel lines. For this reason, we first continue
our study of parallel lines.

\section*{9--2\quad TRANSVERSALS}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{9--2\quad Transversals}

The results of this chapter are based on the important theorem about parallel
lines given below. In the theorem, one of the lines is \emph{between} the other
two. You should be able to tell what this means in terms of the ideas of
betweenness that you have studied.

\exercise{9--1}
If $l$, $l'$, and $l''$ are three parallel lines, what should it mean to say that
$l'$ is between $l$ and $l''$?

\begin{theorem}
If $l$, $l'$, and $l''$ are parallel lines, and $m$ is a transversal intersecting
the lines in points $A$, $B$, and $C$, respectively, such that
$\sg{AB} \cong \sg{BC}$, and if $m'$ is any other transversal intersecting $l$,
$l'$, and $l''$ in $A'$, $B'$, and $C'$, respectively, then
$\sg{A'B'} \cong \sg{B'C'}$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{remark}
A brief way of stating the theorem is to say that if parallel lines cut off
congruent segments on one transversal, then they cut off congruent segments on
every transversal.
\end{remark}

\noindent\emph{Proof.}
\begin{steps}
\step{Consider lines $m''$ and $m'''$ through $A$ and $B$, respectively, and
      parallel to $m'$ in Fig.~9--1.}{Which postulate?}
\step{$m''$ intersects $l'$ in $D$, and $m'''$ intersects $l''$ in $E$.}{Why?}
\step{$A$, $A'$, $B'$, and $D$ are vertices of a parallelogram, as also are $B$,
      $B'$, $C'$, and $E$.}{Since $l$, $l'$, and $l''$ are parallel, as also are
      $m'$, $m''$, and $m'''$.}
\step{$\sg{AD} \cong \sg{A'B'}$, $\sg{BE} \cong \sg{B'C'}$.}{Why?}
\step{$\ang{ABD} \cong \ang{BCE}$,\newline $\ang{BAD} \cong \ang{CBE}$.}{Why?}
\step{$\sg{AB} \cong \sg{BC}$.}{Given.}
\step{$\tri{ABD} \cong \tri{BCE}$.}{A.S.A.}
\step{$\sg{AD} \cong \sg{BE}$.}{Statement 7.}
\step{$\sg{A'B'} \cong \sg{B'C'}$.}{Statements 4 and 8 and properties of
      congruence.}
\end{steps}

If a line is parallel to one side of a triangle and bisects another side, then it

If a line bisects two sides of a triangle, it is parallel to the third side, and

\begin{construction}
Given the segment $AB$, the problem is to divide it into a number of congruent
segments. (In Fig.~9--8, the number is four.)
\end{construction}

\begin{steps}
\step{Draw a ray $AC$, forming an angle with $AB$ that is not a straight
      angle.}{How do you know that such a ray exists?}
\step{With a compass, mark off points $D$, $E$, $F$, and $G$, in order $ADEFG$,
      such that $\sg{AD} \cong \sg{DE} \cong \sg{EF} \cong \sg{FG}$.}{This can be

\step{Draw $BG$.}{Postulate I.}
\step{Draw lines through $D$, $E$, and $F$, $\pll BG$.}{These lines exist by
      Theorem 8--1. The construction is \RC. Why?}
\step{The parallel lines through $D$, $E$, and $F$ intersect $AB$ in points $H$,
      $I$, and $J$, respectively.}{Why?}
\step{$\sg{AH} \cong \sg{HI} \cong \sg{IJ} \cong \sg{JB}$.}{Theorem 9--1 on

\end{steps}

\begin{remark}
In discussing measurement in Chapter 5, we had to divide the unit segment into

\end{remark}

\section*{9--4\quad PROPORTION}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{9--4\quad Proportion}

Before discussing similar triangles, we recall some facts about proportions that
you learned in arithmetic and algebra.

\begin{definition}
If $x$ and $y$ are numbers, with $y \neq 0$, the ratio of $x$ to $y$ is the
number $x/y$, that is, $x$ divided by $y$. A \emph{proportion} is a statement
that two (or more) ratios are equal. For example, $a/b = c/d$ is called a
proportion. In this proportion, we say that $a$ and $c$ are proportional to $b$
and $d$, respectively, and also that ``$a$ is to $b$ as $c$ is to $d$.'' More
generally, the statement that $x$, $y$, $z$, $u$, \dots\ are proportional to
$x'$, $y'$, $z'$, $u'$, \dots\ means that $x/x' = y/y' = z/z' = u/u' = \cdots$.
In all proportions, we must require that the denominators are not zero, because
division by zero is impossible.
\end{definition}

You may recall that in algebra you learned a number of theorems about
proportions, some of which are troublesome to remember. All statements about
proportions are simple consequences of the basic laws of algebra. The theorems on
proportions in this chapter are not to be memorized, but rather their proofs are
to be understood.

We list below some theorems about proportions that are simple consequences of the
laws of algebra. Since a ratio is a number obtained by division, we first recall
the meaning of division.

\begin{definition}
If $b \neq 0$, then $a/b$ (or $a \div b$) is the number $q$, such that $a = qb$.
In other words, for $b \neq 0$, $a/b = q$ if and only if $a = qb$.
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}
If $b \neq 0$, and $c \neq 0$, then $a/b = (ac/bc)$.
\end{theorem}

\noindent\emph{Proof.} If $q = a/b$, then $a = qb$. Therefore, $ac = q \cdot bc$.
Whence $q = (ac/bc)$.

\begin{theorem}
If neither $b$ nor $d = 0$, $a/b = c/d$ if and only if $ad = bc$.
\end{theorem}

\noindent\emph{Proof.} The statement $a/b = c/d$ says that two numbers are equal,
that is, they are the same number. Multiplying this number by $bd$, we have
$bd \cdot a/b = bd \cdot c/d$, or $da = bc$. Conversely, if $ad = bc$ and
$bd \neq 0$, then dividing this number by $bd$, we have $(ad/bd) = (bc/bd)$ or
$a/b = c/d$.

\begin{theorem}
If $c \neq 0$, then $a/b = c/d$ if and only if $a/c = b/d$.
\end{theorem}

\noindent\emph{Proof.} Both proportions are equivalent to $ad = bc$, by
Theorem 9--3.

\section*{9--5\quad RATIO AND PROPORTION IN GEOMETRY}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{9--5\quad Ratio and Proportion in Geometry}

The objects of study in geometry are sets of points. Some of these objects have
numbers attached to them; for example, a segment has a \emph{length}. Later, we
also discuss area of polygons and measurement of angles. In geometry, when we
speak of the ratio of two segments, we mean the \emph{ratio of the numbers} that
are the lengths of the segments. It will be much the same when we speak of the
ratio of any other geometric objects.

\begin{definition}
If $AB$ and $CD$ are segments, the ratio of $AB$ to $CD$ is the number
$\sg{AB}/\sg{CD}$. The statement that the segments $AB$ and $CD$ are
\emph{proportional} to the segments $A'B'$ and $C'D'$ means that
$\sg{AB}/\sg{A'B'} = \sg{CD}/\sg{C'D'}$. We say that the sides of $\tri{ABC}$ are
proportional to the sides of $\tri{A'B'C'}$ if $\sg{AB}/\sg{A'B'} =
\sg{BC}/\sg{B'C'} = \sg{CA}/\sg{C'A'}$.
\end{definition}

\FIGIXFIFTEEN

Before proceeding, we should observe that a question might be raised concerning
Definition 9--3. The number that we get for the length of a segment depends on
what we choose for a segment of unit length. Thus a foot ruler has length 1 if
the unit length is one foot, but has length 12 if the unit is one inch. We now
justify Definition 9--3 by proving that the ratio does not depend on the unit of
length.

\begin{theorem}
No matter what unit of length is chosen for measuring two segments, the ratio of
these segments is the same number.
\end{theorem}

\noindent\emph{Proof.} Suppose that the two segments are $AB$ and $A'B'$, and
suppose that we have two different unit segments $CD$ and $EF$, as in Fig.~9--16.

\FIGIXSIXTEEN

\noindent Suppose the lengths of $AB$ and $A'B'$, when $CD$ is the unit, are $l$
and $l'$ respectively, and their lengths, when $EF$ is the unit, are $L$ and $L'$
respectively. We have seen, in Theorem 5--4, that changing the unit of measure
just multiplies the length by a number. That is, if the length of $CD$, when $EF$
is the unit, is $x$, then $L = l \cdot x$, and $L' = l' \cdot x$. Hence, if we
form the ratio of $AB$ to $A'B'$ when $CD$ is the unit, we get $l/l'$; and if we
form the ratio when $EF$ is the unit, we get
\[
  \frac{L}{L'} \;=\; \frac{l \cdot x}{l' \cdot x} \;=\; \frac{l}{l'}.
\]
Hence, the two ratios are the same, and the theorem is proved.

We have defined a ratio to be a number, and numbers can be either \emph{rational}
or \emph{irrational}. When we encounter these two kinds of ratios in geometry, we
use the words \emph{commensurable} and \emph{incommensurable}. We inherit this
language from the Greeks, who discovered irrational numbers and were much
disturbed by their discovery. We make the language clear in the following
definition.

\begin{definition}
One segment is said to be \emph{commensurable} with another if the ratio of the
first to the second is a rational number. They are said to be
\emph{incommensurable} if this ratio is an irrational number.
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}
Two segments, $AB$ and $CD$, are commensurable with each other if and only if
there is a segment $EF$ which, if used as a unit of measure, makes $\sg{AB}$ and
$\sg{CD}$ whole numbers (integers).
\end{theorem}

\begin{remark}
Such a segment $EF$ is a common unit of measure for the two segments, hence the
word \emph{commensurable}.
\end{remark}

\noindent\emph{Proof.} We have to show that if such a segment $EF$ exists, then
$\sg{AB}/\sg{CD}$ is a rational number, and conversely, that if
$\sg{AB}/\sg{CD}$ is a rational number, then such a segment $EF$ exists.

Suppose first that such a segment $EF$ exists. Then $\sg{AB} = m$, and
$\sg{CD} = n$, where $m$ and $n$ are integers. Therefore $\sg{AB}/\sg{CD} = m/n$,
and is a rational number.

To prove the converse, suppose that $\sg{AB}/\sg{CD}$ is a rational number, that
is, $\sg{AB}/\sg{CD} = m/n$, where $m$ and $n$ are integers. Divide the segment
$CD$ into $n$ equal subintervals, and call one of these subintervals $EF$
(Fig.~9--17). With $EF$ as a unit of measure, $\sg{CD} = n$, and $\sg{AB} = m$.

\FIGIXSEVENTEEN

\begin{note}
In this book, our theorems deal only with commensurable pairs of segments, and we
assume their truth for incommensurable cases. Proofs for incommensurable cases
require some knowledge of \emph{limits}. The ideas involved are difficult and we
avoid limits whenever possible.
\end{note}

\begin{histremark}
Pythagoras (6th century B.C.) believed that all quantitative relations could be
described by pure numbers. By this, he meant whole numbers, or integers. He was
(so the story goes) profoundly shocked when he discovered that the side and
diagonal of a square are incommensurable. Because of this discovery, later Greeks
(notably Eudoxus and Euclid) invented an elaborate theory of ratio and
proportion that is a model of ingenuity and precision. The interesting fact
about this theory, from our point of view, is
that \emph{there were no numbers in Greek geometry, that is, no measurement}, and
hence the meaning of the words ``two ratios are equal'' had to be defined in a
special way that we cannot consider here.
\end{histremark}

\section*{9--6\quad SIMILAR TRIANGLES AND POLYGONS}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{9--6\quad Similar Triangles and Polygons}

In this section we are primarily concerned with similar triangles, but we also
define similarity for polygons, and solve some problems concerning pairs of
similar polygons. Remember that we are trying to make precise what we mean when
we say that two polygons \emph{have the same shape}.

\begin{definition}
Two \emph{triangles} are said to be \emph{similar} to each other if corresponding
angles are congruent and corresponding sides are proportional. For example, in
Fig.~9--18, $\tri{ABC}$ is similar to $\tri{A'B'C'}$ if $\ang{A} \cong \ang{A'}$,
$\ang{B} \cong \ang{B'}$, $\ang{C} \cong \ang{C'}$, and $\sg{AB}/\sg{A'B'} =
\sg{BC}/\sg{B'C'} = \sg{CA}/\sg{C'A'}$. To indicate that triangles are similar,
we write $\tri{ABC} \simto \tri{A'B'C'}$.
\end{definition}

Two \emph{convex} polygons are said to be \emph{similar} to each other if the
corresponding angles are congruent and the corresponding sides are proportional.
For example, the polygons in Fig.~9--19 are similar if $\ang{A} \cong \ang{A'}$,
$\ang{B} \cong \ang{B'}$, $\ang{C} \cong \ang{C'}$, $\ang{D} \cong \ang{D'}$,
$\ang{E} \cong \ang{E'}$, and $\sg{AB}/\sg{A'B'} = \sg{BC}/\sg{B'C'} =
\sg{CD}/\sg{C'D'} = \sg{DE}/\sg{D'E'} = \sg{EA}/\sg{E'A'}$.

\section*{9--7\quad THEOREMS ON SIMILAR TRIANGLES}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{9--7\quad Theorems on Similar Triangles}

In this section we give the fundamental theorems on similar triangles. They are
extremely important because they have wide application in mathematics, other
sciences, and engineering.

\begin{theorem}
If the angles of one triangle are congruent, respectively, to the angles of
another triangle, then the triangles are similar.
\end{theorem}

\noindent\emph{Proof.} Suppose that the triangles are $\tri{ABC}$ and
$\tri{A'B'C'}$, as in Fig.~9--22, and that $\ang{A} \cong \ang{A'}$,
$\ang{B} \cong \ang{B'}$, and $\ang{C} \cong \ang{C'}$. We must prove that the
sides are proportional, that is, $\sg{AB}/\sg{A'B'} = \sg{BC}/\sg{B'C'} =
\sg{CA}/\sg{C'A'}$.

\FIGIXTWENTYTWO

In forming the proof, we will suppose that the segments $AB$ and $A'B'$ are
commensurable. In other words, $\sg{AB}/\sg{A'B'}$ is a rational number. The
proof for the incommensurable case is difficult, since it involves knowing about
\emph{limits}. We therefore suppose that, with a suitable unit, the lengths of
$AB$ and $A'B'$ are positive integers. The figure is drawn with $\sg{AB} = 5$ and
$\sg{A'B'} = 3$. We may suppose that $AB > A'B'$, for if $AB < A'B'$, the
argument can be reversed, and if $AB \cong A'B'$, the triangles are congruent
(why?), and hence similar.

\begin{steps}
\step{There is a point $A''$ on $AB$, such that $\sg{A''B} \cong \sg{A'B'}$, and
      $\sg{A''B} = \sg{A'B'} = 3$.}{Congruence postulate.}
\step{$A''$ is between $A$ and $B$.}{We have assumed that $AB > A'B'$.}
\step{There is a line through $A''$ parallel to $AC$ which intersects $BC$ in
      $C''$.}{Why?}
\step{$\tri{BA''C''} \cong \tri{B'A'C'}$.}{A.S.A.}
\step{$\sg{BC''} \cong \sg{B'C'}$.}{Statement 4.}
\step{There are points $D$, $E$, and $F$, on $AB$, such that the points $A$, $F$,
      $A''$, $E$, $D$, and $B$, in that order, are at unit distance from their
      neighbors.}{It is given that $\sg{AB} = 5$. By Statement 1,
      $\sg{A''B} = \sg{A'B'} = 3$.}
\step{There are lines through $D$, $E$, and $F$, parallel to $AC$. These lines
      intersect $BC$ in points $I$, $H$, and $G$, respectively.}{Why?}
\stepx{8}{$\sg{CG} \cong \sg{GC''} \cong \sg{C''H} \cong \sg{HI} \cong
      \sg{IB}$.}{Theorem 9--1 on transversals.}
\stepx{9}{If $CG$ is chosen as unit of length, $\sg{BC} = 5$, and $\sg{BC''} =
      \sg{B'C'} = 3$.}{Definition of length and Statement 5.}
\stepx{10}{$\sg{BC}/\sg{B'C'} = 5/3$.}{Statement 9.}
\stepx{11}{$\sg{AB}/\sg{A'B'} = 5/3$.}{Given.}
\stepx{12}{$\sg{AB}/\sg{A'B'} = \sg{BC}/\sg{B'C'}$.}{Statements 10 and 11.}
\stepx{13}{In an analogous way, by considering a line parallel to $BC$, it may be
      shown that $\sg{AB}/\sg{A'B'} = \sg{AC}/\sg{A'C'}$.}{Same reasons as in
      Statements 1 through 12.}
\stepx{14}{$\tri{ABC} \simto \tri{A'B'C'}$.}{Statements 12 and 13.}
\end{steps}

\begin{remark}
It should be clear that the proof is essentially the same if $AB$ and $A'B'$ are
any other positive integers besides 5 and 3.
\end{remark}

\begin{corollary}
If two angles of one triangle are congruent to two angles of another triangle,
then the triangles are similar.
\end{corollary}

\begin{corollary}
If an acute angle of one right triangle is congruent to an acute angle of another
right triangle, then the triangles are similar.
\end{corollary}

\section*{9--8\quad THE PYTHAGOREAN THEOREM}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{9--8\quad The Pythagorean Theorem}

With our knowledge of similar triangles, we are able to prove a theorem
associated with the Greek mathematician Pythagoras, who lived in the 6th century
B.C. It is believed that he first gave a proof of the theorem that bears his
name. First, we state a theorem that is useful for the proof of the Pythagorean
theorem.

\begin{theorem}
The altitude to the hypotenuse of a right triangle forms two right triangles,
which are similar to the given triangle. For example, in Fig.~9--40,
$\tri{ABC} \simto \tri{ACD} \simto \tri{CBD}$.
\end{theorem}

\FIGIXFORTYFORTYONE

\noindent\emph{Proof.} In the first place, a line through $C$ perpendicular to
$AB$ must intersect the segment $AB$. This fact may be seen as follows: Consider
lines $l$, $l'$, and $l''$ in Fig.~9--41, through $C$, $A$, and $B$, and
perpendicular to $AB$. Then $l' \pll l \pll l''$. The point $C$ is between $l'$
and $l''$, because angles $A$ and $B$ are acute angles. Therefore, $l$ is between
$l'$ and $l''$, and hence $l$ intersects $AB$ in a point $D$. $\tri{ACD} \simto
\tri{ABC}$, since each triangle has a right angle, and the triangles have the
acute angle $A$ in common. By the same reasoning, $\tri{BCD} \simto \tri{BAC}$.

\begin{corollary}
The altitude to the hypotenuse is a mean proportional\textsuperscript{*}
between the two segments formed on the hypotenuse.
\end{corollary}
\IXstarnote{*$x$ is a ``mean proportional'' between $a$ and $b$ if
$a/x = x/b$, or, which is the same, $x^2 = ab$.}

\noindent\emph{Proof.} We have to show that, using Fig.~9--40,
$\sg{AD}/\sg{CD} = \sg{CD}/\sg{DB}$. Since $\tri{ADC} \simto \tri{CDB}$, this is
an immediate consequence of the definition of similar triangles.

\begin{corollary}
A leg of a right triangle is the mean proportional between the hypotenuse and the
adjacent segment on the hypotenuse formed by the altitude from the right angle.
That is, in Fig.~9--40, $\sg{AC}^2 = \sg{AD} \cdot \sg{AB}$.
\end{corollary}

\begin{theorem}[Pythagorean theorem]
The square of the length of the hypotenuse of a right triangle is equal to the
sum of the squares of the lengths of the legs.
\end{theorem}

If the triangle is $\tri{ABC}$, with the right angle at $C$, and if the lengths
of the sides opposite angles $A$, $B$, and $C$ are $a$, $b$, and $c$,
respectively, as in Fig.~9--46, then we are able to show that $c^2 = a^2 + b^2$.

\FIGIXFORTYSIX

\noindent\emph{Proof.}
\begin{steps}
\step{Let $CD$ be the altitude and $x = \sg{AD}$ and $y = \sg{DB}$. Then
      $x/b = b/c$ and $y/a = a/c$.}{Corollary 9--11--2.}
\step{$x + y = c$.}{Properties of length.}
\step{$b^2/c + a^2/c = c$.}{Statements 1 and 2.}
\step{$a^2 + b^2 = c^2$.}{Statement 3 and algebra.}
\end{steps}

\begin{theorem}[Converse to Pythagorean theorem]
If a triangle has sides of length $a$, $b$, and $c$, such that $c^2 = a^2 + b^2$,
then the triangle is a right triangle, and the side of length $c$ is the
hypotenuse.
\end{theorem}

\noindent\emph{Proof.} The method of proof is to show that there is a right
triangle that is congruent to the given triangle. Suppose that the given triangle
is $\tri{ABC}$.

      postulates.}

\section*{9--9\quad ON CONSTRUCTIONS}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{9--9\quad On Constructions}

In our previous study of \RC\ constructions, we assumed that two circles with
equal radii would intersect if the distance between their centers were less than
the sum of their radii. We can easily prove this by using the Pythagorean
theorem. To do this, we first calculate where they \emph{should} intersect, and
then prove that this point \emph{is} on both circles.

\FIGIXFIFTYFIFTYONE

Consider Fig.~9--50, where $r$ is the radius of the circles, and $d = \sg{AB}$ is
the distance between their centers. If the circles intersect at $P$, and $C$ is
the midpoint of $AB$, then
$\tri{PAC} \cong \tri{PBC}$ (why?), and hence $\ang{C}$ is a right angle.
Therefore, $r^2 = \sg{PC}^2 + \sg{AC}^2$, so that
$\sg{PC} = \sqrt{r^2 - (\frac{1}{2}\sg{AB})^2}$. We now know ``where'' the point
of intersection $P$ must be, and we can prove that such a point of intersection
exists.

\begin{theorem}
Suppose that circles, with centers $A$ and $B$, have equal radii,
$r > \frac{1}{2}AB$. These circles intersect.
\end{theorem}

\noindent\emph{Proof.} Let $C$ be the midpoint of $AB$. Through $C$ there is a
line perpendicular to $AB$. On this line, let $P$ be a point at the distance
$\sg{PC} = \sqrt{r^2 - (\frac{1}{2}\sg{AB})^2}$ from $C$. (How do we know there
is such a point $P$?) Because $\tri{PAC}$ and $\tri{PBC}$ are right triangles, it
follows from the Pythagorean theorem that $\sg{PA} = \sg{PB} = r$. Hence $P$ lies
on both circles.

\begin{remark}
It also follows from the above proof that the two circles can intersect in two,
and only two, points, for the following reason. On the line through $C$,
perpendicular to $AB$, there are only two points at the correct distance from
$C$, namely one on each side of the line $AB$.
\end{remark}

\begin{theorem}
An angle inscribed in a semicircle is a right angle. That is, if in Fig.~9--51,
$AB$ is a diameter of the circle, with center at $O$, and $C$ is any point on the
circle other than $A$ and $B$, then $\ang{ACB}$ is a right angle.
\end{theorem}

\noindent\emph{Proof outline.} We drop a perpendicular from $C$ to $AB$, and
prove that the right triangles $ADC$ and $CDB$ are similar. It then follows that
$\ang{C}$ is a right angle.

\begin{steps}
\step{Let $CD$ be the perpendicular from $C$ to $AB$, and suppose that $D$ is
      between $A$ and $O$.}{The point $D$ must be between $A$ and $B$. (Why?)
      $D$, then, either is $O$, or is between $A$ and $O$, or $O$ and $B$. If
      $D = O$, the proof is easy. We discuss only the case in which $D$ is
      between $A$ and $O$. If $D$ is between $O$ and $B$, a similar proof can be

\section*{REVIEW OF CHAPTER 9}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Review of Chapter 9}

Be sure you know the definitions given in this chapter of the following concepts:
transversal, ratio, proportion, ratio of segments, proportional sides,
commensurable, similar triangles.

Some of the important theorems of the chapter were concerned with these
definitions. Try to recall the theorems.

The main ideas of the chapter are listed briefly below. Be sure that you know
them, and review the proofs.

\begin{exlist}
\item If parallel lines cut off congruent segments on one transversal, they do so
      on every transversal.\\
      This theorem depended upon what facts about parallel lines and triangles?\\
      This theorem enables one to divide a segment into $m$ congruent parts with
      ruler and compass. Show how to do this.
\item What is a proportion? What is the ratio of $x$ to $y$ if $x$ and $y$ are
      numbers? if $x$ and $y$ are segments?\\
      If neither $b$ nor $d$ is zero, $a/b = c/d$ if and only if $ad = bc$.
      Illustrate this theorem by numerical examples.
\item The ratio of two segments does not depend on the unit of length.\\
      Illustrate this fact by getting the ratio of depth to width of your desk,
      first measuring in feet and then in inches.\\
      On what theorem about measurement of length does this fact depend?
\item[\stex 4.] Two segments are defined to be commensurable with each other if
      their ratio is a rational number. What is a rational number? Give an
      example of one. Give an example of an irrational number. What are
      incommensurable segments?\\
      State a theorem concerning commensurable segments.
\item[5.] What is the definition of similar triangles and polygons? Draw two
      similar triangles; pentagons.\\
      What do an architect's drawings have to do with similar figures?\\
      What does a map have to do with similarity?
\item[6.] Two triangles with corresponding angles congruent are similar.\\
      Illustrate the proof of this theorem, using two similar triangles $ABC$
      and $A'B'C'$, with $AB = 12$, and $A'B' = 8$.
\item[7.] If two triangles have their corresponding sides proportional, they
      are similar.\\
      What must be established to prove this theorem?
\item[8.] The Pythagorean theorem is proved, using a theorem about similar
      triangles in a right triangle. State this theorem.\\
      Prove the Pythagorean theorem.\\
      State its converse.
\end{exlist}
